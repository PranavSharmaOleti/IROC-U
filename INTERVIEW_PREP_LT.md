## 3. Your scripts (memorise, then improvise)

### 3.1 The 90‑second internship answer (memorise this verbatim)

> "My internship was a plant training attachment at BHEL Hyderabad, in the Turbines, Compressors and Gas Turbine division. I was not given design ownership — it was a guided visit with the division's technical run-down, and I took notes on everything I saw.
>
> Three things stuck with me. One: the rotor of a steam turbine is not designed, it is machined — a large alloy-steel forging goes through a fixed sequence of nineteen operations on the centre lathe and horizontal boring machine, blades are fitted into grooves, shrouding is welded on, and the rotor is balanced stage-wise before the casing closes over it. The biggest machine in that plant is dedicated to first-job rotor balancing, because imbalance force goes as the square of speed.
>
> Two: the drawing is only a third of the job. The process route and the inspection gate at each operation are the other two-thirds — the casing's horizontal joint is located by taper pins and has to be colour-matched by scraping before it is accepted.
>
> Three: I saw a twelve-metre-long job machined in one setup on a 170 kW heavy-duty CNC lathe — centre height 2100 mm, 4200 mm swing without the telescopic cover, and a 4.2 m diameter job only fits without that cover. I wrote down the machine specifications and the tooling logic too, because I wanted to understand why they choose solid, tipped or indexable inserts over one another.
>
> What I took from it: I now think of engineering as a chain from process plan to inspection gate, not as a drawing on a screen. And it is why I want to be in heavy manufacturing rather than design-only work."

**Why this works:** it is 100% true, it contains three checkable specifics, it converts observation into
insight, and it gives the panel a natural hook to pull — which is what you want, because a panel that
asks a follow-up has already decided you are worth talking to.

### 3.2 "What did you learn?"
> "Three things I could not have got from a textbook. First, that a turbine rotor is not 'designed' — it is machined from a forging through a fixed 19-operation sequence, and every operation has a tolerance and an inspection gate, so process planning is really the discipline of turning a drawing into a shop sequence. Second, metrology: the difference between 0.01 mm and 0.002 mm changes whether a labyrinth seal works. Third, that balance is everything — the biggest lathe in BHEL is dedicated to first-job rotor balancing, because residual imbalance scales with the square of speed."

### 3.3 "Why did you intern at BHEL if you want to join L&T?"
> "BHEL was deliberate — it is where India's turbine capacity is actually built, and I wanted to see heavy manufacturing rather than only read about it. That experience is what took me from wanting to be a designer to wanting to be a mechanical engineer who can actually take a component from the drawing to the shop floor and close it out. L&T is where I want to do that at scale — L&T builds nuclear, power and defence equipment with its own heavy engineering capability, and it is a bigger, more diverse platform than a single PSU unit."

### 3.4 "What was your actual contribution? / Did you do any real work?"
> "Training and observation, not design ownership. I was attached to BHEL Hyderabad's TC & GT division for a plant training visit. I was taken through the steam turbine and compressor shops, the foundries, the tool room, the heat treatment shop and the CNC machining area, and BHEL gave our batch a technical run-down on the division's product range and processes — I studied that and took notes on everything I saw. What I produced was my own notes; what I took away was a picture of how a drawing actually becomes a machined, measured, balanced rotor."

### 3.5 "What is your weakness?"
> "CNC part programming and reading a GD&T drawing to specification. I know the vocabulary from the shop floor, but I have never programmed a part end-to-end or owned a tolerance stack-up. That is exactly what I want to learn in the GET programme, and it is why I am joining a company that manufactures rather than only designs."
